What a car build really costs

The parts cart is roughly 60% of what you will actually spend. The rest is tax, shipping, consumables, labor, the bolt that snapped, and the running costs that start the day the car is back together. Here is the whole list, itemised, so you can price a build before you commit to it instead of after.

A worked example

Say the plan is a downpipe, a cat-back and a tune. The cart says $2,400. Written out in full, with a shop doing the install, it looks like this:

LineAmount
Parts (downpipe, cat-back, tune licence)$2,400
Sales tax at 7%$168
Oversize freight$120
Gaskets, hardware, clamps, anti-seize$95
Fluids and one step colder plugs$130
Install labor, 6 hours at $130$780
While you are in there: seized bolt + one sensor$210
Dyno time for the final tune$175
Total$4,078

The parts were 59% of the build. Nothing on that list is exotic or unlucky — every line is the normal case. The numbers above are an illustration, not a quote; the point is the shape, and the shape holds whether your build is $800 or $18,000.

The lines people leave out

  • Sales tax and shipping. On heavy or oversize parts, freight alone can be 5% of the cart.
  • Consumables. Gaskets, bolts, clamps, fluids, sealant, a fresh set of plugs. Small individually, 3–6% of a build in total, and always paid at the worst moment.
  • Tools you do not own yet. If you are doing it yourself, the spring compressor or the torque wrench is part of this build even though it pays back on the next one.
  • Labor, including the labor you are not being charged for. A weekend is a real cost even when nobody invoices you for it.
  • The while-you-are-in-there tax. Budget 5–10% of the build for the thing you find once it is apart. It is not pessimism; it is the median outcome.
  • Tuning and validation. Dyno time, a second pull after the fix, an alignment after anything suspension-related.
  • Downtime. If the car is your commute, a week on jack stands has a price in rides, rentals or missed shifts.

The costs that start after the build

A build is not a one-time number. It changes your monthly costs, and this is the part that quietly wrecks budgets:

  • Fuel. Higher octane on the same miles is a permanent monthly line, not a one-off.
  • Wear. Stickier tires and more power mean tires and brakes arrive sooner. A set that used to last three years arriving in eighteen months doubles that monthly set-aside.
  • Insurance. Declared modifications can change a premium, and undeclared ones can change what a claim pays out. Ask before, not after.
  • The next part. Be honest about whether this is the last one. Most builds are a subscription, and the healthy version of that is a monthly number you chose.

How to price a build before you start

  1. Build the parts cart, with real part numbers.
  2. Add tax and shipping at checkout values, not estimates.
  3. Add 5% for consumables and 8% for the while-you-are-in-there tax.
  4. Add labor at your shop's real hourly rate times an honest hour count, or a weekend of your own time.
  5. Write down the new monthly running cost separately. That number goes in your budget, permanently.

Then decide. A build you priced properly and chose is a hobby. A build that arrived one part at a time on a credit card is a debt with a spoiler on it.
